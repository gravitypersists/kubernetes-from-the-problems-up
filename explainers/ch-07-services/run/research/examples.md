# Examples

### x-01
objective: o-2
example: The entry's analogy: a team's shared inbox or front desk. You write to orders@, not to a person; whoever is on shift picks it up. People join, leave and go home, and the address doesn't change. The receptionist's rota (who is in today) is kept current by someone whose only job is updating it.
why: Makes the stable name, the changing members, and "you contact the team, not a person" concrete. Where it breaks: (1) a front desk is a person who handles every message, but a Service is a rule carried out on each caller's own node, with nobody in the middle (m-10, c-05); (2) the rota-keeper is the EndpointSlice controller (c-03), but it doesn't judge who is fit to work: it copies each Pod's own readiness answer (local-01, c-04); (3) a shared inbox lets whoever is free pick the next message, but a Service picks a Pod at random when the connection opens, without knowing who is busy (local-02). The second and third breaks are exactly the chapter's two local misconceptions, so the analogy earns its place only if the breaks are drawn.
source: CURRICULUM.md §7 Ch 7 (analogy); breaks from c-03, c-04, c-05

### x-02
objective: o-1
example: Candidate cause for Fennel's "connection refused" incidents that fits the established story: `orders` runs a single copy (Ch 4, Ch 5). On Ch 5's Tuesday, `orders-q2rw` was OOM-killed three times and "customers see errors for a moment each time". While its container was down and restarting, the only `orders` Pod wasn't ready, so the `orders` Service's list was empty, and every call from `storefront` got "connection refused" at once (c-04, c-06). If `orders` has no readiness probe, the restarted Pod is listed again as soon as its container runs, possibly before the app is listening, so a few more calls are refused (Ch 6 claim 07). The ordinary fix is two or more copies plus a readiness check, not a different Service.
why: Explains an established fact (Ch 5's "errors for a moment") instead of inventing a new outage, keeps the Services-have-been-working-all-along continuity (the brief's story question), and teaches the chapter's key move: when no Pod is ready, the Service has nowhere to send traffic, so the fix is in the Pods, not the Service. Alternatives the designer can weigh: (a) a new internal tool whose Service selector has a typo (`app: order` vs `app: orders`) matches no Pods: refused on every call while every Pod shows Running and Ready, a vivid "everything looks green" case (debug page: "If the `ENDPOINTS` column is `<none>`, you should check that the `spec.selector` field..."); in practice this happens on a newly written Service, because a Deployment's own selector can't be edited after creation (model knowledge, not separately sourced); (b) a caller that copied a Pod's address (e.g. from a dashboard) and kept it after a replacement: usually timeouts, not refusals (c-06 note), so it fits "connection refused" less well; (c) Ch 3's by-hand era: `storefront` pointing at dead machine 03 would mostly have seen timeouts, too. The story facts themselves are the designer's to propose and the user's to approve.
source: CURRICULUM.md §3 (Ch 4–5 story facts); c-04, c-06; Ch 6 claim 07

### x-03
objective: o-2
example: What `storefront` sees during a `catalog` release (Ch 6's three copies, default rolling update, readiness check `/healthz`): the `catalog` Service's list starts as three v63 addresses; a fourth Pod (v64) starts, and its address is added when it passes readiness; an old v63 Pod is told to stop and its address drops out at once (terminating); this repeats until the list holds three v64 addresses. At every moment the list has at least three ready addresses (Ch 6 claim 03), and in the middle it mixes versions, so consecutive calls from `storefront` can reach v63 and v64. During Ch 6's 14:02 release, customers would have seen some $0 prices before the rollout finished.
why: Connects Ch 6's lane to the Service's list, step by step, with the loop doing the bookkeeping Sam did by hand. It shows that "zero downtime" depends on the list staying non-empty and only holding ready Pods, and that "old and new serve side by side" is a property of the list. It also supports the brief's out-of-scope line: steering a chosen share of traffic (a canary) needs more than Pod counts, because the list picks at random.
source: c-03, c-04, c-05; Ch 6 claims 03 and 06; CURRICULUM.md §3 Ch 6

### x-04
objective: o-3
example: Load-balancer cost, one provider (AWS, US East N. Virginia, Application Load Balancer, prices read 2026-10-24): $0.0225 an hour × 730 hours ≈ $16.43 a month per load balancer before usage. A separate load balancer for each of Fennel's six services (`storefront`, `orders`, `payments`, `dispatch`, `catalog`, `notifications`) ≈ 6 × $16.43 ≈ $98.60 a month in hourly charges alone, against ≈ $16.43 for one shared gateway, plus usage either way (AWS's own small example totals $22.42 a month for one). At Year 4's "30+ services" it would be 30 × $16.43 ≈ $493 a month in hourly charges. Only services customers reach need one at all: internal callers use cluster IPs (local-03).
why: Gives Morgan-style real numbers and shows the money is modest at Fennel's size: the larger costs of many load balancers are many public addresses to secure, certify, monitor and keep track of, and many separate settings. Label it as one provider's figure at a date; other providers price differently (some per rule or per data processed), and Ch 14 owns cross-provider cost.
source: c-12 (https://aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/pricing/); CURRICULUM.md §3 (services), §3 Timeline (Year 4)

### x-05
objective: o-3
example: One customer request, traced: a phone opens `fennel.example` → the provider's cloud load balancer (one public address, the provider's machine) → the gateway or ingress (one entry point; its rules say `fennel.example/` goes to the `storefront` Service and, say, `/api/catalog` to `catalog`) → the `storefront` Service (a cluster IP and a list of ready Pods; the rule on the node picks one at random) → one ready `storefront` Pod → which then calls `orders` by its DNS name, `orders`, inside the cluster, again through a Service and never through a load balancer.
why: Makes o-3's trace concrete with four things that differ in kind: a provider machine, a routing configuration with a controller behind it, a rule kept by a loop, and a Pod. The second hop (`storefront` → `orders`) shows internal traffic needs no outside load balancer. The `/api/catalog` route is illustrative, not an established story fact.
source: c-07, c-08, c-09, c-05

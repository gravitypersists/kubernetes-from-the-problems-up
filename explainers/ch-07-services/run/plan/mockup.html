Finding each other: services and traffic

For a somewhat technical manager (novice for infrastructure) · about 20 minutes planned (likely 22–25 built) · narrative article with one scrollytelling lane, three gated reveal figures and static figures · Chapter 7 of 15

Standfirst (draft): Fennel's services call one another all day, and customers call storefront from their phones, while the Pods behind every service keep being replaced. This chapter shows how a call still finds its way, why storefront's calls to orders were refused one Tuesday, and what it costs to let customers in.

Shape: Told from the caller's side. The opener recalls Sam passing on new addresses by hand (Ch 3) and states the manager's puzzle: Tuesday's connection refused lines. The felt pain is a prediction, not a widget: a hand-copied list of catalog's addresses after Thursday's rollback (k-02 → Fig. 01). The Service is shown as what Ari wrote and what Kubernetes adds (Fig. 02) before it is named. The one lane replays Ch 6's 10:15 release with one new thing drawn, the catalog Service's list of ready Pods, which the endpoints loop keeps while storefront's setting never changes. Then three predict-then-reveals: no server in a call's path (k-04 → Fig. 03, m-10), Tuesday's empty list (k-05 → Fig. 04), and a customer's request traced (k-06 → Fig. 05). Rejected: the entry's hands-on address-list widget (pain already felt in Ch 3; tapping is busywork, Q6); a lane for Tuesday (one moment; two snapshots show it); two lanes (+1.5 min).

Objectives:

  • o-1 Explain why callers need a stable name and address when Pods are disposable (every replacement and every release, rollbacks included, brings new addresses; a hand-kept list is always behind), and predict what a caller using a Pod's address gets after a replacement. checks: k-01, k-02
  • o-2 Describe how a Service connects callers to whichever Pods are ready: a stable name and address, a selector, a list of ready Pods kept by the endpoints loop; a record carried out as rules on every node, not a server (m-10); old and new side by side in a rollout; refused at once when no Pod is ready. checks: k-03, k-04, k-05
  • o-3 Trace a customer's request (cloud load balancer → gateway → Service → ready Pod; internal calls need no load balancer) and weigh one load balancer per service against one gateway, with what each costs. checks: k-06, k-07

Minutes: hero + §01 2.5 · §02 1.75 · §03 2.25 · §04 lane 3.5 · §05 2.5 · §06 2.0 · §07 3.0 · §08 0.75 · §09 1.75 · about 20 (checks ≈ 3.75, lane ≈ 1.75). Ch 6 carried the same load and was measured at about 27 against 22 planned, so expect 22–25 built; the cut order below recovers about 3.

s-03

One name that stays put: the Service

artefact → termo-2

Ground the Service before naming it: what Ari wrote at the move and what Kubernetes adds; then the term, the two meanings of "service", and the DNS name.

Beside each service's Deployment, Ari wrote one more short record. For orders: a name, orders; a selector, app: orders, which says which Pods belong (any Pod with that label, which orders' Deployment gives every Pod it makes); and the port callers use.

Kubernetes gives the record an address of its own, 10.96.0.25 (engineers call it the cluster IP), which stays the same however often the Pods change. The cluster's DNS answers the name orders with it. And it keeps a list of the addresses of the matching Pods that are ready; since 03:10 in Chapter 4, one: orders-q2rw's.

TERM 01 · Service (payoff variant): "A record that gives a set of Pods, picked out by a selector, one name and address that stay the same while the Pods change, with a list of which of them are ready." Back: "What storefront has been calling since the move."

At Fennel, orders is a service, a program that runs as Pods; each also has a Service of the same name, the record. Code font for the program, capital S for the record.

The name a caller uses is a DNS name, like a website's, answered only by the cluster's own DNS. D2 (collapsed) "What's the full name, and why doesn't DNS just list the Pods?"

s-04

Thursday, 10:15, from storefront's side

scrollytelling B1, gatedo-2

On Ch 6's lane R scene, show the endpoints loop keeping catalog's list current while storefront's setting never changes; fill in its lens; it checks nothing itself; side by side and the canary.

Set-up: This is Thursday's 10:15 release from Chapter 6 again, when catalog went from v62 to v63 in about a minute. This time the record panel shows the catalog Service: its name and address at the top, which storefront uses, and under them its list of ready Pods. Keeping that list is the job of one more loop, running in the control plane since the move: it watches every Service's selector and the Pods that match, and rewrites the list whenever one becomes ready, stops being ready, or goes. Engineers call the list the Service's endpoints and the loop the EndpointSlice controller; here it's the endpoints loop.

S1 · 10:15:00 The list holds three v62 addresses; storefront names only catalog → 10.96.0.21.

S2 · seconds later The Deployment loop starts a v63 Pod on 05; it matches the selector but isn't ready, so it isn't listed and no call reaches it. → k-03

S3 · ~10:15:20 (after k-03) v63 ready → on the list within seconds, usually; a v62 Pod told to stop → off at once, before it exits. Answers come from both versions.

S4 · ~10:16 Every address on the list has changed; the line at the top, which storefront uses, didn't once.

Lens: wants each Service's list to hold exactly the ready Pods its selector matches · sees selectors, matching Pods, which are ready · does: rewrites the list. It put orders-q2rw on the list at 03:10 in Chapter 4, and took Chapter 6's catalog Pod off when it lost its database.

It checks nothing itself (local-01): it copies each Pod's readiness answer; a Pod with no readiness check is listed once its container runs; the $0 v64 Pods passed and were listed.

Side by side: why some customers saw $0 before 14:02's rollout finished. And a canary by Pod count is rough: one v64 Pod in three gets roughly a third of new connections, at random; exactly one in twenty takes routing rules a plain Service doesn't have.

s-05

Where a call actually goes

misconception → predict → reveal → analogyo-2

Confront m-10: no server in the middle; kube-proxy on every node writes the rules; spread at random, not by load; the support-line analogy with its breaks.

Fair statement: Ari's team runs everything that matters in two or more copies, and every call from storefront to orders uses the orders Service. It has an address and a name, as a server does, and at Fennel "service" has always meant a program that runs. It's natural to picture a box in the middle.

(Fig. 03 and the reveal collapse until k-04 is answered.) The address belongs to no machine or program. On every node, a small loop called kube-proxy reads each Service and its list and writes them into the node's own forwarding rules; the caller's node sends each connection straight to a listed Pod. Nothing called "the orders Service" can crash, slow down or need copies; the Pods need copies. Something does run (the endpoints loop; kube-proxy), but neither sits on a call's path. D3 "What if kube-proxy stops?"

Spread, not balanced: by default a listed Pod is picked at random each time a connection opens, without knowing how busy it is. D4 "Why does one Pod get most of the calls?" One sentence names service mesh (Chapter 13).

ANALOGY aside: A team's support line: customers call one number, not a person's phone, and the phone system rings one of the people on today's rota; people come and go, the number never changes. A Service is the number and the rota together. WHERE THIS ANALOGY BREAKS: a support line runs through a switchboard every call passes, and someone decides who is fit to be on the rota. A Service has neither: each caller's own node connects the call straight to a Pod, and the endpoints loop judges nobody; it lists whichever Pods pass their own readiness checks.

PREDICT · k-04
Every call from storefront to orders uses the orders Service, and Ari's team wants to be sure the Service itself can't become the weak point. How many copies of the orders Service should they run?
A · Two or more, on different nodes, as for any service that every order depends on.
B · One is enough: if it crashed, Kubernetes would restart it, as it would any Pod.
C · None: each caller's own node sends the call straight to an orders Pod. (answer)
Tempting: A and B (m-10: a Service is a server).

s-06

Tuesday, 12:40: connection refused

incident → explain → revealo-2

Answer §01's puzzle: an empty list refuses calls at once; a restart keeps the Pod's address; the fix is in the Pods.

orders runs a single copy, orders-q2rw on node 04. On Tuesday at about 12:40 its memory reached its limit, the operating system killed its container, and the node agent restarted it in place (Chapter 5). For those seconds, storefront's log shows connection refused.

(Fig. 04 and the explanation collapse until k-05 is answered.) While its container was down and starting again, the only Pod wasn't ready, so the endpoints loop took it off the list: nowhere to send calls. With an empty list, the rule on each caller's node refuses a call at once rather than holding it, in most clusters, by default. Seconds later the Pod was back on the list at the same address: a restart keeps the Pod, and its address. The same at 18:50 and 20:10.

The fix is in the Pods, not the Service: two copies on different nodes, and one restarting Pod leaves the other on the list. Ari proposes a second copy. Jordan's question: "Which of our services run a single copy, and what do their callers get while it restarts?" D5 "Other ways to get 'connection refused'" (a selector that matches nothing; no readiness check; a stale address usually times out instead).

s-07

From a customer's phone to a Pod

two options → terms → trace → costo-3

Inside only by default; a cloud load balancer per Service vs. one gateway for many; Fennel's set-up; trace a request; what each costs.

Every Service so far can be reached only inside the cluster, by default. That's all storefront needs to reach orders, with no load balancer. Customers' phones are outside.

One way in per Service: set its type to LoadBalancer, and the provider creates a load balancer for that one Service, a machine it runs, with a public address and its own hourly charge. TERM 02 · load balancer: "A machine the cloud provider runs, with a public address, that passes incoming connections on to what's behind it; a Service of type LoadBalancer gets one of its own." Back: "The provider's machine, billed by the hour."

One way in for many: routing rules that send each web request to a Service by the name or path it asks for, carried out by a controller (the provider's, or one the team installs and keeps up to date), usually behind one cloud load balancer. Older form: an ingress; newer, recommended by the Kubernetes project: a gateway. TERM 03 · gateway (or ingress): "One entry point for a cluster's web traffic, whose rules send each request to a Service by the name or path it asks for; a controller carries the rules out." Back: "One way in, for many Services."

Fennel: since the move, one gateway (provider's controller) behind one cloud load balancer; its only route sends fennel.example to storefront.

(Fig. 05 collapses until k-06 is answered.) Cost, labelled: on one provider, AWS, an Application Load Balancer in one US region was listed at $0.0225 an hour on 24 October 2026, about $16 a month before use; six would be about $99 against about $16 for one; at thirty services, about $490. Other providers price differently. The bigger cost: each one is another public entry point to secure, give a certificate, watch and track. Real reasons for more than one exist (non-web traffic; keeping traffic apart). D6 "Ingress or gateway?" (Ingress frozen; Ingress NGINX retired 24 March 2026; who keeps our controller up to date?)

A planning meeting. Partners' apps want to read catalog's prices directly, and Riley's team wants control over how they get in.

Recap (five lines): new Pods, new addresses, so callers use a name · a Service: a stable name and address for the Pods a selector picks out · the endpoints loop keeps its list of ready Pods, copying each Pod's own answer · kube-proxy turns lists into rules on every node; a call goes straight to one Pod, at random; no server between · an empty list refuses calls; the fix is more ready Pods · from outside: a load balancer per Service of that type, or one gateway for many.

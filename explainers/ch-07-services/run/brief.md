## Objectives

- o-1: after reading, the reader can explain why callers need a stable name and address when Pods are disposable: every replacement (Ch 4) and every release (Ch 6) gives a service new Pods with new addresses, and a hand-kept list of addresses (Sam's, Ch 3) is always behind; and can predict what a caller that uses a Pod's address directly will see when that Pod is replaced.
- o-2: after reading, the reader can describe how a Service connects callers to whichever Pods are currently ready: a stable name and address that callers use, a selector that says which Pods belong, and a list of the ready Pods' addresses that a loop keeps current (filling in the loop lens row Ch 4 and Ch 6 left dashed); that the Service is a routing rule carried out on every node, not a server that runs (m-10); and what callers see during a rollout and when a Pod stops being ready.
- o-3: after reading, the reader can trace at a high level how a customer's request from the internet reaches a Pod (a cloud load balancer, a gateway or ingress that routes by name or path, a Service, a ready Pod), and weigh one load balancer per service against one gateway in front of many, including what each costs.

## Research questions

- q-1 (o-1, o-2): What a Service is, at a manager's level: a stable virtual address and name; a selector on Pod labels; the EndpointSlice controller (which loop it is, what it watches, that it lists only ready Pods by default, and what happens to Pods that are shutting down); kube-proxy or its equivalent on each node turning the Service into routing rules (so no Service process exists to fail, m-10); how traffic is spread across Pods by default (random or round-robin, not by load); how quickly a change (a new ready Pod, a Pod that fails readiness) reaches callers. What does a caller see during a rolling update (old and new Pods both in the list)?
- q-2 (o-1, o-2): What goes wrong without a Service or around one: a caller using a Pod's address directly when the Pod is replaced; traffic reaching a Pod before it's ready (no readiness check); a selector that matches no Pods (an empty list, and what a caller sees: refused or timed out connections); long-lived connections that stay on one Pod. Which of these produce "connection refused", and which timeouts? Pick what fits Fennel's "connection refused" incidents.
- q-3 (o-2): DNS names inside the cluster: what name a caller uses (`orders`, and the full form with a namespace, kept light), who keeps the name pointing at the Service's address, and why DNS points at the Service, not at each Pod (and the exception, headless Services, for Ch 10, named only).
- q-4 (o-3): Getting traffic in: a Service of type LoadBalancer asks the cloud provider for a load balancer (one per Service); Ingress and the Gateway API (one entry point routing by host name or path to many Services; which is current, the state of the Ingress API and of widely used ingress controllers as of 2026, verified); what the managed provider runs vs. what the team installs. Cost: one major provider's load-balancer price (hourly plus data), labelled with provider and date, and what one per service adds up to for Fennel's handful of services.
- q-5 (all): What do managers and newcomers get wrong about Services and traffic? Evidence for m-10 ("a Service is a server"); candidates for new `local-NN` entries, e.g. "a Service checks whether Pods are healthy" (it relies on readiness), "a Service balances by load", "every service that talks to another needs a load balancer". Service mesh: one accurate sentence on what it adds, to name it correctly.

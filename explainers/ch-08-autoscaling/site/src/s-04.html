§ 04 The Friday of the push

The cluster looks different now that both loops are running. Within an hour of their being switched on, the HPA had taken dispatch from six copies down to three, the cluster autoscaler had emptied node 05 and given it back, and Fennel was renting four nodes instead of five; how the loops decide to shrink things is the subject of a later section. On an ordinary Friday the rush builds over about an hour from five o’clock. dispatch climbs from three copies to six as it does, the three extra Pods all fit on node 06, which is nearly empty, and nobody at Fennel notices any of it.

The following widget steps through a Friday that was not ordinary. Its drawing is the cluster from earlier chapters, with a few additions. The record shows only the line the HPA writes, dispatch’s number of copies, with one mark for each Pod, filled once that Pod is ready; the words beside the marks count the Pods that are ready and any that are waiting for room, so a Pod that is still starting is one of the hollow marks left over. Under the nodes, a line gives what the HPA measures, next to its target, and another says what customers are seeing. Both new loops have a pill beside the ones you know, and the one that is acting is lit. On the nodes, a Pod drawn with only its name is ready, because there is rarely room for the word; a Pod that isn’t ready always says so, with STARTING in place of its name where the chip is small. A Pod with no room waits in a strip above the nodes, as in Chapter 5, and a node that has been asked for but isn’t ready yet is drawn as a dashed frame.

It was the Friday before a holiday weekend. At 17:30, half an hour into the rush, Fennel’s marketing team sent every customer a notification offering free delivery, and told nobody on Ari’s team that it was coming.

The decision was the quick part. Within a minute of the reading crossing its target, the HPA had asked for nine copies, and the scheduler and the cluster autoscaler each did their part seconds later. What cannot be hurried is everything the decisions set in motion. A Pod has to download its image, start its program and pass its readiness check before it takes any work, and a rented machine has to start and join the cluster before a Pod can even begin. As a rule of thumb, Pods that fit on room the cluster already has are typically working within a minute or two, and Pods that need a new node within roughly three to eight minutes, depending mostly on the provider. You have seen this shape before: when node 03 died in Chapter 4, its Pods were replaced in about six minutes, and there too each loop acted within seconds while the time went on the waiting in between. So the common belief that autoscaling is instant is half right. The deciding is close to instant, and the arriving is not.

Nothing failed that Friday, and seven minutes is what it looks like when everything works. It can take longer. If the provider has no machine of that kind free at that moment, the request typically fails and is tried again after a wait that is itself measured in minutes. And if the pool is already at its maximum, the loop doesn’t ask at all, and the Pods wait until a person raises the number.

Could it take twenty minutes?

It could, when delays stack up. On one provider, Google’s GKE, the first retry of a failed request for a node comes after five minutes, and the waits between retries can grow to thirty (its documentation, November 2026). In June 2026 AWS described a customer whose very large scale-up took 23 minutes because requests for machines kept failing and being retried. And by default the cluster autoscaler gives a node it has asked for up to 15 minutes to appear before it gives up on it. Twenty minutes is what several delays in a row look like. It isn’t what autoscaling normally costs.

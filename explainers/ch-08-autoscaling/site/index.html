I didn’t read the last chapter. What happened?
  • Fennel, a grocery-delivery company, runs its services on managed Kubernetes. Kubernetes keeps a record of what Fennel wants running, such as “3 copies of dispatch”, and many small loops each compare one part of that record with what is really running, and act when the two differ. To understand any loop, ask four things: which loop is it, what does it want (the desired state), what does it see (the real state), and what does it do when the two differ?
  • Chapter 5: each copy of a service runs in a Pod, and each Pod reserves memory and processor on a node, one of the rented machines in the cluster. Those reservations are its requests. The scheduler, the loop that gives each new Pod a node, places it by what it reserves, never by what it uses. A Pod with no room waits, and Fennel pays for every node it rents, busy or not.
  • Chapter 6: a Pod takes work only once it is ready, which means it has started and passed its readiness check.
  • Chapter 7: each service has a Service, a stable name and address in front of its Pods, whose list holds the Pods that are ready.

On a Friday in Chapter 5, the dispatch team raised dispatch from 3 copies to 6, and one of the new Pods waited, Pending, for about an hour because no node had room for it. What ended the wait?

That Friday left its mark on the cluster. dispatch, the service that assigns drivers to orders, has run six copies ever since, every hour of every day, although it needs six for only about two hours a week: between five and seven on a Friday evening, when Fennel’s orders triple. On an ordinary evening the work would keep about two copies busy. Nodes 05 and 06, rented in a hurry that evening, are still in the cluster too, which is how Morgan came to ask in Chapter 5 why Fennel pays for five nodes that are less than a third busy.

Nobody chose this waste on purpose. Two decisions sit behind it, and people make both of them by hand. How many copies of dispatch to run is the dispatch team’s decision, written as a number in dispatch’s Deployment. How many nodes to rent is a decision for Ari’s team. Each was made once, for the busiest hour of the week, and then left alone, because changing either one means somebody remembering to do it twice every Friday.

Jordan“Can’t the cluster add copies when it’s busy, and take them away when it isn’t?”

Ari“It can. Two loops do it between them. Neither is as quick as you’d hope.”

Each of those two decisions can be handed to a loop of its own. This chapter takes them one at a time, and then watches them work together on the worst evening they have had so far.

§ 02 A loop that changes a number

Start with the first decision, the number of copies, and with something Kubernetes can measure. Each dispatch Pod reserves one processor core, as its team wrote in its requests in Chapter 5. An ordinary evening’s work, shared among three copies, would take about 0.4 of a core from each, which is 40% of what each reserved; at a Friday rush, with too few copies, the same Pod might be asked for more than its whole core. Kubernetes can read that share for every Pod. It is a share of the Pod’s own reservation, not of the machine, so a service whose team never set a request gives this measurement nothing to stand on.

The dispatch team, with Ari, puts that measurement to work. Beside dispatch’s Deployment they add one more short record, which says three things: keep the average across dispatch’s Pods at about 60%, never run fewer than 3 copies, and never run more than 10. The record switches on a loop that works for dispatch alone, and engineers call a loop of this kind an autoscaler. The figure below shows the record on the left and, on the right, what the loop makes of three different readings.

The rule the loop follows is one of proportion. It compares the reading with the target and scales the number of copies by the same ratio, rounding up. Take the first reading in the figure: six copies, each at 90%. That is one and a half times the target, so the work would sit at the target if it were spread over one and a half times as many copies, and six become nine. The two limits fence the result in. The loop never goes below the minimum, however quiet the evening, and at the maximum it simply stops, however high the reading climbs.

The loop’s full name is the one on the first line of the team’s record: the HorizontalPodAutoscaler. Engineers say “the HPA”, and so will the rest of this chapter.

New term · 01 / 03

HorizontalPodAutoscaler · HPA

A loop that keeps one measurement of a service’s Pods near a target by raising or lowering the number of copies the service wants, between a minimum and a maximum.

Horizontal: more Pods, not bigger ones.

The HPA is one more implementation of the reconcile loop from Chapter 4, running in the control plane beside the others, and the loop lens fits it as it fits them. What it wants is its service’s Pods, on average, at the target. What it sees is how much processor each of those Pods is using. When the two differ, it rewrites one number in the record: the copies its Deployment wants. Everything that turns that number into running Pods is the work of loops you already know. The ReplicaSet loop writes the new Pod records, the scheduler gives each one a node, and the node agents start them.

Two practical things follow from how it measures. The first is its pace: by default the HPA looks about every fifteen seconds, and it leaves the number alone while the reading is within a tenth of the target, so small wobbles change nothing. The second is that requests matter again. Because the reading is a share of the request, a padded request of the kind Chapter 5 warned about makes a busy Pod look idle, and the loop acts late. The measurement doesn’t have to be processor use, though that is the common choice.

Does it have to be processor use? And other kinds of autoscaling

No. An HPA can read memory instead, or a measurement the team defines for itself, and when it is given several it works out a number for each and takes the largest. Where those measurements come from is a subject for Chapter 12.

You’ll also hear two other kinds of autoscaling named. Vertical autoscaling changes each Pod’s requests instead of the number of Pods; unlike the HPA, it isn’t built into Kubernetes and has to be installed separately. Event-driven scaling, for which KEDA is the usual tool, scales a service on events such as the length of a queue, and can take it all the way down to zero copies.

§ 03 A loop that rents machines

The HPA’s work ends at the record. When it raises dispatch’s number, the new Pods are placed like any others, by their requests, on whichever nodes have room.

That leaves the second decision, the number of nodes. Managed Kubernetes offers a loop for it as well, usually as a setting that the platform team switches on: the cluster autoscaler, or your provider’s equivalent. It can do the two things that only people at Fennel could do before, which are to ask the provider for another node and to hand one back. Ari’s team switched it on in the same week that dispatch got its HPA.

The cluster autoscaler watches for one thing: Pods that the scheduler has tried to place and couldn’t. It never looks at how busy the nodes are, for the same reason the scheduler doesn’t. Room in a cluster is counted in reservations, as Chapter 5 showed, so a node at 85% is not full as long as its Pods’ requests fit, and a new node would change nothing for Pods that already have one. The guess that it reacts to busy machines isn’t far off, because the first layer does: the HPA starts from a reading of use, and busy Pods are what make it ask for more copies. The second layer reacts only to what the first one leaves behind, which is Pods without a node.

That one trigger explains two earlier evenings. On the Friday in Chapter 5, the nodes were full on paper, one new dispatch Pod had no room, and it waited for an hour until Ari’s team rented two more machines. Had this loop been running, that Pod would have been given a new node without anyone on Ari’s team having to notice it and rent one. But the loop would not have replaced node 03 when that machine died in Chapter 4, because every Pod from node 03 found room on the three nodes that were left, and so nothing was waiting.

Before it asks for a node, the loop works out whether a new one would actually give the waiting Pods room, which means it has to know what a new node would be: how much memory, how many cores. That is the job of a node pool, a group of identical machines that the cluster can add to or shrink. Fennel has one, made of the 16 GB, 4-core machines it has rented all along, and Ari’s team gave it a minimum of 3 nodes and a maximum of 8. The loop moves the pool between those two numbers and never past them. The maximum in particular is a person’s decision, and it works as a cap on spending: when the pool reaches it, the loop stops asking, and any Pod that still has no room simply waits.

New term · 02 / 03

cluster autoscaler

A loop that asks the provider for another node when a Pod has no room, and hands a node back when its Pods all fit elsewhere.

The loop Chapter 5 was missing.

New term · 03 / 03

node pool

A group of identical machines in a cluster, with a smallest and a largest size that people set; nodes are added to it and removed from it.

Some providers say node group.

A cluster can have several pools, one for each kind of machine, and the two dashed pools in the figure are there to show why it might. If orders ever outgrew the 13 GB that a general node can give its Pods, no number of general nodes would help, as Chapter 5 found, and the loop could add nothing useful until someone had defined a pool of bigger machines. Ari has also proposed a pool of spot machines, which cost less because the provider can take one back at short notice (AWS, for one, gives two minutes’ warning, as of November 2026). A service that can be interrupted, such as notifications, could live there. orders and payments would not.

Through the loop lens, the cluster autoscaler wants every Pod on a node, on no more nodes than that takes. It sees the Pods the scheduler couldn’t place, and how much of each node is reserved. When those differ from what it wants, it adds a node from a pool, up to the pool’s maximum, or it empties a node it can do without and gives it back. How carefully it does the second of those comes later in the chapter.

My engineers say Karpenter, or node auto-provisioning. Is that this loop?

It is the same job done by a different tool, and the names differ by provider. Some newer tools skip the pre-made pools: Karpenter, and the modes that providers build on it, such as “node auto-provisioning” on Microsoft’s AKS and Auto Mode on AWS’s EKS. They pick a machine to fit the Pods that are waiting, within limits the team sets, and they tend to start nodes faster. What they react to is the same: Pods with no room. Some providers go a step further and manage the nodes for you; Google’s GKE Autopilot is one.

§ 04 The Friday of the push

The cluster looks different now that both loops are running. Within an hour of their being switched on, the HPA had taken dispatch from six copies down to three, the cluster autoscaler had emptied node 05 and given it back, and Fennel was renting four nodes instead of five; how the loops decide to shrink things is the subject of a later section. On an ordinary Friday the rush builds over about an hour from five o’clock. dispatch climbs from three copies to six as it does, the three extra Pods all fit on node 06, which is nearly empty, and nobody at Fennel notices any of it.

It was the Friday before a holiday weekend. At 17:30, half an hour into the rush, Fennel’s marketing team sent every customer a notification offering free delivery, and told nobody on Ari’s team that it was coming.

The decision was the quick part. Within a minute of the reading crossing its target, the HPA had asked for nine copies, and the scheduler and the cluster autoscaler each did their part seconds later. What cannot be hurried is everything the decisions set in motion. A Pod has to download its image, start its program and pass its readiness check before it takes any work, and a rented machine has to start and join the cluster before a Pod can even begin. As a rule of thumb, Pods that fit on room the cluster already has are typically working within a minute or two, and Pods that need a new node within roughly three to eight minutes, depending mostly on the provider. You have seen this shape before: when node 03 died in Chapter 4, its Pods were replaced in about six minutes, and there too each loop acted within seconds while the time went on the waiting in between. So the common belief that autoscaling is instant is half right. The deciding is close to instant, and the arriving is not.

Nothing failed that Friday, and seven minutes is what it looks like when everything works. It can take longer. If the provider has no machine of that kind free at that moment, the request typically fails and is tried again after a wait that is itself measured in minutes. And if the pool is already at its maximum, the loop doesn’t ask at all, and the Pods wait until a person raises the number.

Could it take twenty minutes?

It could, when delays stack up. On one provider, Google’s GKE, the first retry of a failed request for a node comes after five minutes, and the waits between retries can grow to thirty (its documentation, November 2026). In June 2026 AWS described a customer whose very large scale-up took 23 minutes because requests for machines kept failing and being retried. And by default the cluster autoscaler gives a node it has asked for up to 15 minutes to appear before it gives up on it. Twenty minutes is what several delays in a row look like. It isn’t what autoscaling normally costs.

§ 06 Scaling down, and Morgan’s bill

The way down is slower than the way up, and it is slower on purpose. On the Friday of the push, orders began to ease at about seven. The HPA saw the reading fall straight away, but by default it lowers the number of copies only as far as the highest number it wanted at any moment in the last five minutes, so that a brief dip in orders doesn’t remove copies the next minute will need. dispatch started to come down at about 19:05 and was back at three copies by about 19:20.

Nodes come down more slowly still, because removing Pods doesn’t remove a node. The cluster autoscaler gives a node back only when three things hold, by default: the Pods on it reserve less than half of its room, every one of those Pods would fit on some other node, and both have stayed true for about ten minutes.

Fennel went from five nodes to four, which is about a fifth fewer node-hours in a week, against work that falls by two-thirds on a quiet day. The copies do follow the work closely, but Fennel doesn’t pay for copies. It pays for nodes, and nodes come and go as whole machines. Underneath them sits a floor that no amount of quiet will lower: the minimums that people chose, the services that have no autoscaler at all, and every request, because a node full of padded reservations never looks empty to this loop. That is Chapter 5’s lesson about padding, with a second cost attached. A cluster with many more nodes than Fennel’s typically moves in finer steps, but its floor is made of the same things.

What else stops a node being given back?

Several things, with default settings. A Pod that no loop would replace keeps its node, since removing it would lose it for good. So can a Pod that stores files on the node’s own disk, and so can a team’s rule limiting how many copies of a service may be down at once (these are called disruption budgets, and Chapter 14 comes back to them). Timing matters too: by default, no node is given back within about ten minutes of one being added.

§ 07 catalog’s slow Sunday

When a service is slow because it has too few copies for its work, more copies fix it. That was dispatch on the Friday of the push, and once the copies arrived the slowness was gone. The catalog team drew the natural conclusion and gave catalog, the service behind Fennel’s product listings and prices, an HPA of its own, with the same 60% target and a minimum of three copies.

It was tested on the Sunday evening of the holiday sale. For about a quarter of an hour, catalog’s pages took whole seconds to load. The cause was not in catalog’s own code: each request was waiting for an answer from the database that catalog reads its prices from. That database is not one of the cluster’s Pods in this story; where a database should run is a question for Chapter 10.

An HPA knows one measurement and one target, and nothing else. It doesn’t know how many customers are waiting, or how long, or what they are waiting for. A Pod that is waiting on a database is barely using its processor, so on that Sunday the one number the loop could see said that all was well. Autoscaling doesn’t fix slowness in general. It fixes the kind of slowness that shows up in the measurement it was given.

Riley’s first thought that evening was to do by hand what the loop had not, and raise catalog to nine copies. The right-hand panel of the figure shows why that would have gone badly. The database accepts 100 connections at a time, which is PostgreSQL’s typical default, and each catalog copy holds up to 30 of them, so three copies already hold 90. Nine copies would ask for 270. A fourth copy would get the last ten connections and limp along on a third of what it wants. The other five couldn’t connect at all, so they would fail the readiness check that asks whether they can reach the database, the one from Chapter 6, and would never appear on the list of catalog’s Service; meanwhile they would be competing for connections with the three copies that were working. These numbers are an illustration, but the arithmetic is the general one: copies, times connections per copy, against a limit that doesn’t move. The fix was on the database’s side, which is Chapter 10’s subject.

The rule is short. Adding Pods helps only when Pods are what’s short. So the question to ask of any plan to autoscale a service is this one:

“What is the bottleneck after the app?”

The question to ask

A database is one answer, and there are two more worth knowing. payments calls a payment provider that accepts only so many calls a second from Fennel, a ceiling known as a rate limit, and more payments Pods just means more callers under the same ceiling. And the work has to reach the new Pods in the first place. A Service picks a Pod when a connection opens, as Chapter 7 showed, so callers that keep their connections open for a long time stay with the old Pods, and new copies can stand nearly idle until those connections are reopened.

§ 08 The loops so far

Scaling was the last of Sam’s jobs without a loop. It now has two: one changes how many copies a service wants, and one changes how many nodes there are to put them on.

ANSWERTwo questions matter most.

“How fast do we scale, compared with how fast the traffic rises?” Fennel has measured its own answer, counted from the first late order on the Friday of the push: about three minutes until the copies that fitted on room it already rents were working, and about seven for the copies that needed a new node. A rush that builds over an hour is fine; one that arrives within a few minutes, like the push, is not, so for the days Fennel can see coming, ask what the minimums and the spare room will be beforehand and who will raise them.

“What is the bottleneck after the app: the database, the payment provider?” More payments Pods don’t raise the number of calls the payment provider accepts, and more storefront or orders Pods lean on databases that have limits of their own, as catalog showed; orders running in more than one copy is a question in itself (Chapter 10).

Then the settings: “What are the target, the minimum and the maximum for each service, and the node pool’s maximum? Who chose them, and what happens when we reach one?” At a maximum the loops stop and Pods wait; the maximum is also the cap on what a bad day can cost.

Last, for Morgan: “What will the holidays cost, and what does it fall back to afterwards?” The bill follows nodes, and it comes down only as far as nodes can be emptied.

Sam’s sentence rests on a common belief, that autoscaling is instant and fixes any slowness. What you have seen instead is two loops that take minutes and act on one measurement each, so being ready is still something people plan.

In short

  • The HPA reads one measurement of a service’s Pods and rewrites the number of copies wanted, between a minimum and a maximum that people chose.
  • The cluster autoscaler adds a node when a Pod is waiting for room, never because nodes are busy, and a node pool sets which kind of machine it adds and how many at most.
  • Chained together, the two take minutes, and most of those minutes go on starting machines and Pods.
  • Every way of shortening that wait costs money or effort.
  • On the way down, copies fall cautiously and a node goes back only once every Pod on it fits elsewhere, which gives the bill a floor.
  • More Pods help only when Pods are what’s short.

A year on, Fennel has six teams and thirty services, and payments has to run in staging and in production with different settings and different keys. Where those settings and keys live, and who can read them, is Chapter 9.

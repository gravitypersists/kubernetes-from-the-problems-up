s-02 · §02

A loop that changes a number

worked example → applyo-1

Teach the HPA alone: what it measures, what the team writes for it, its rule, and that all it does is rewrite a number.

Each dispatch Pod reserves one processor core. On an ordinary evening a copy uses about 0.4 of it: 40% of what it reserved. Kubernetes can read that share for every Pod.

The dispatch team, with Ari, adds one short record beside dispatch's Deployment: keep the average at about 60%; never fewer than 3 copies; never more than 10.

The loop compares the reading with the target and scales the number of copies by the same ratio, rounding up: six copies reading 90%, one and a half times the target, become nine. At the maximum it simply stops.

Apply · k-02
It's 17:50 on a Friday. dispatch has 3 copies, and its autoscaler (target 60%, minimum 3, maximum 10) reads an average of 120%. What does the autoscaler do?
  • A · Adds one copy, waits to see the effect, and adds another if the reading is still high.
  • B · Changes the number of copies wanted to 6, and leaves the rest to other loops.
  • C · Starts three new dispatch Pods itself, on whichever node is emptiest.

New term · HorizontalPodAutoscaler (HPA): a loop that keeps one measurement of a service's Pods near a target by raising or lowering the number of copies the service wants, between a minimum and a maximum.

Through the loop lens: it wants its service's Pods, on average, at the target; it sees what each Pod is using; when the two differ it rewrites one number. The ReplicaSet loop, the scheduler and the node agents do the rest.

By default it looks about every fifteen seconds. It reads a share of the request, so a padded request makes a busy Pod look idle. Collapsed: "Does it have to be processor use? And other kinds of autoscaling" (names vertical and event-driven scaling).

s-03 · §03

A loop that rents machines

predict → revealo-1

Teach the cluster autoscaler alone: what it watches (not what you'd guess), and how a node pool tells it what it may add.

When the HPA raises the number, the new Pods are placed like any others, by their requests. On the Friday in Chapter 5 that is where things stopped: one Pod had no room and waited an hour.

Managed Kubernetes offers a loop for that last step, usually as a setting the platform team switches on: the cluster autoscaler, or your provider's equivalent. It can ask the provider for another node and hand one back.

Predict · k-03
Fennel's cluster now has a loop that can rent another node. On a busy evening every Pod has a node, and the four nodes' processors are 85% busy. What does that loop do?
  • A · Rents a node now, before the processors run out.
  • B · Nothing: no Pod is waiting for room.
  • C · Moves a few Pods over to whichever node is least busy.

(Locked until the check is answered.) It watches for Pods the scheduler has tried and failed to place. It never looks at how busy the nodes are, because the scheduler doesn't either. The HPA does start from a reading of use; this loop reacts only to Pods without a node.

It would have ended that hour's wait in Chapter 5 in minutes. It would not have replaced node 03 in Chapter 4: every Pod found room on the three nodes left, so nothing waited.

Before asking for a node, the loop has to know what a new node would be. A node pool is a group of identical machines that the cluster can add to or shrink. Fennel has one, with a minimum of 3 nodes and a maximum of 8. The maximum is a person's decision and a cap on spend: at it, the loop stops and Pods wait.

New terms · cluster autoscaler (a loop that asks the provider for another node when a Pod has no room, and hands a node back when its Pods all fit elsewhere) · node pool (a group of identical machines in a cluster, with a smallest and a largest size that people set).

If orders ever outgrows a 13 GB node, someone has to define a pool of bigger machines first. Ari has also proposed a pool of spot machines, cheaper because the provider can take one back at short notice; notifications could live there, orders and payments would not.

Collapsed: "My engineers say Karpenter, or node auto-provisioning. Is that this loop?"

s-04 · §04

The Friday it took seven minutes

scrollytelling lane, gatedo-1o-2

Show both loops chained on one evening with every wait visible; confront "autoscaling is instant" at the gate; then lay out where the minutes went.

Set-up. Within an hour of the two loops being switched on, dispatch went from six copies to three and node 05 was emptied and given back, so the cluster now has four nodes. On an ordinary Friday the rush builds over an hour, dispatch goes from three copies to six, and the extra Pods fit on node 06; nobody notices.

On the Friday before a holiday weekend, at 17:30, Fennel's marketing team sent every customer a notification offering free delivery, and told nobody on Ari's team.

Lane steps (text beside the drawing):

A1 · 17:30. dispatch runs its minimum of three copies, each using about half the processor it reserves. The push goes out.

A2 · ~17:34. Within four minutes each copy is using nearly twice what it reserved: 180%, three times the target. Three times the target means three times the copies, so the HPA rewrites the number from 3 to 9. That is all it does.

A3 · seconds later. The ReplicaSet loop writes six new Pod records, and the scheduler places them by what they reserve. Node 06 has room for three. The other three wait, as one did in Chapter 5.

Predict · k-04 (unlocks A4–A6)
Three dispatch Pods are waiting for room, and this time Fennel's cluster has a loop that can rent a node. About how long until those three Pods are taking work?
  • A · A few seconds: adding a node is automatic now, and the Pods start on it at once.
  • B · A few minutes: the machine has to start, and then the Pods on it.
  • C · About an hour, as in Chapter 5, because someone on Ari's team still has to approve the new node.

A4 · ~17:36. Seconds after the Pods began to wait, the cluster autoscaler worked out that one more node from the general pool would hold all three, and asked the provider for it. That machine is still starting. Meanwhile the three Pods on node 06 are ready, so six copies share the work.

A5 · ~17:38:30. About four minutes after it was asked for, node 07 joins the cluster: a new machine with a new name. The scheduler places the three waiting Pods on it at once.

A6 · ~17:40. All nine copies are ready and the reading is back at its target. From the first late assignment to this moment took about seven minutes.

Jordan, on Monday: "We have autoscaling. Why were we slow?" Ari: "Autoscaling worked. It took seven minutes."

The decision was the quick part. Pods that fit on room already there are typically working in a minute or two; Pods that need a new node in roughly three to eight minutes, depending mostly on the provider. Chapter 4's replacement Pods took about six minutes for the same kind of reason.

Nothing failed that Friday. If the provider has no machine of that kind free, the request is tried again after a wait of minutes; if the pool is at its maximum, the Pods wait until a person raises it. Collapsed: "Could it take twenty minutes?"

s-05 · §05

Buying back the minutes

comparison → applyo-2

Give what teams do about the wait, each with what it shortens and what it costs; apply it to a new planned rush.

None of the waits can be removed, but each can be paid down, and each payment is different.

Apply · k-05
Fennel plans a TV advert for next Friday at 19:00, and expects a rush like the push's within a minute or two. Which change gets dispatch ready for 19:00 at the lowest cost?
  • A · Raise the maximum from 10 copies to 20, so the autoscaler has room to go higher.
  • B · Raise the minimum to 9 at 18:45 for that evening, then put it back.
  • C · Lower the target from 60% to 30% from now on, so dispatch always has copies to spare.

Marketing now gives Ari's team a day's notice of any push, and dispatch's minimum is raised for the hour around it. The cheapest fix was a conversation.

Aside · A staffing rule, and where it breaks. The HPA is a standing rule for a support team: keep everyone about 60% busy, never fewer than three people on shift, never more than ten. New people need a desk; when the office is full, renting more space takes far longer than calling someone in, and that is the cluster autoscaler. Where this analogy breaks: a manager would have known the push was coming, and would notice if the team were slow because everyone was waiting on the same broken tool. The loop knows one number and has no judgment.

s-06 · §06

Scaling down, and Morgan's bill

predict → reveal → before and aftero-4

Show that the way down is slower on purpose, let the reader predict which node goes back, and explain why the bill fell by a fifth and not by two-thirds.

That Friday, orders eased from about seven. By default the HPA lowers the number only as far as the highest it wanted in the last five minutes, so a brief dip doesn't remove copies the next minute needs. dispatch was back at three by about 19:20.

Removing Pods doesn't remove a node. The cluster autoscaler gives a node back only if its Pods reserve less than half of it, every one of them fits on another node, and that has stayed true for about ten minutes, by default.

Predict · k-06
By 19:20 the rush is over and dispatch is back at 3 copies. Node 07 is empty. Node 06 holds one small catalog Pod, which reserves 2 GB of the node's 13 GB. Nodes 01, 02 and 04 each have 1 GB free. Which nodes does the cluster autoscaler give back?
  • A · Both: node 07 is empty and node 06 nearly so, and neither is worth its rent.
  • B · Only node 07: the Pod on node 06 has no other node to go to.
  • C · Neither: the loop keeps the nodes it has in case the next rush needs them.

(Locked until the check is answered.) Node 07 went back at about 19:30, after two hours on the bill. Node 06 stays for the reason it stayed in the first week: its one Pod reserves 2 GB and no other node has more than 1 GB free.

Morgan: "Orders drop by two-thirds on a Tuesday. Why doesn't the bill?" Ari: "Because we pay for nodes, and a node goes back only when everything on it fits somewhere else."

Fennel went from five nodes to four: about a fifth fewer node-hours. The copies follow the work closely; the bill follows nodes, in whole machines. Under it sits a floor: the minimums people chose, the services that don't scale at all, and every request, since a node full of padded reservations never looks empty to this loop.

Collapsed: "What else stops a node being given back?"

s-07 · §07

When more Pods don't help

predict → revealo-3

Confront "autoscaling fixes any slowness": the limit was the database, the HPA saw idle processors, and more copies would have made it worse.

When a service is slow because it has too few copies for its work, more copies fix it; that was dispatch. So the catalog team gave catalog an HPA of its own, with the same 60% target.

On the Sunday evening of the holiday sale, catalog's pages took seconds to load for about a quarter of an hour. Each request was waiting for an answer from the database catalog reads its prices from.

Predict · k-07
catalog has an autoscaler like dispatch's: a target of 60% of the processor each Pod reserves. One Sunday evening catalog's pages take seconds to load, because every request is waiting for the database to answer. What does catalog's autoscaler do?
  • A · Adds copies, as it did for dispatch, because a service that is slow needs more Pods.
  • B · Nothing: Pods that are waiting use little processor, so the reading stays low.
  • C · Adds capacity to the database, since that is where the requests are waiting.

(Locked until the check is answered.) The HPA knows one measurement and a target. It doesn't know how many customers are waiting or what they're waiting for.

Riley's first thought was to raise catalog to nine copies by hand. The database accepts 100 connections at a time, PostgreSQL's typical default, and each copy holds up to 30, so three copies already hold 90. Nine would ask for 270. The extra copies couldn't connect, would fail their readiness check, and would never be listed by catalog's Service. The fix was on the database's side, which is Chapter 10's subject.

Adding Pods helps only when Pods are what's short. The question to ask of any plan to autoscale: what is the bottleneck after the app?

Two more places the limit can sit: the payment provider accepts only so many calls a second from Fennel, however many payments Pods are calling; and callers that keep long-lived connections stay on the old Pods (Chapter 7), so new copies stand nearly idle until connections reopen.

The conversation

think, then revealo-2o-3o-4

Rehearse the questions a well-informed manager asks, then recap and look ahead.

Apply · k-08 · think, then show the answer
At the holiday planning meeting, Sam says: "We'll turn on autoscaling, so we're ready for the holidays." storefront, payments and orders have no autoscaler yet. What would you ask, and why? Think of your questions, then show the answer.

Model answer (hidden until revealed): "How fast do we scale, compared with how fast the traffic rises?" Fennel has measured it: a minute or two for copies that fit on room it already rents, about seven when a node has to be added. A rush that builds over an hour is fine; one that arrives in two minutes is not, so ask what the minimums and spare room will be beforehand, and who will raise them. "What is the bottleneck after the app: the database, the payment provider?" More payments Pods don't raise the number of calls the provider accepts, and more storefront or orders Pods lean on databases with limits of their own. "What are the target, minimum and maximum for each service, and the node pool's maximum? Who chose them, and what happens when we reach one?" And for Morgan: "What will the holidays cost, and what does it fall back to afterwards?"

Recap: the HPA reads one measurement and rewrites a number, within limits people chose · the cluster autoscaler adds a node when a Pod is waiting, never because nodes are busy, of a kind and up to a limit a node pool sets · chained, they take minutes, mostly starting machines and Pods · every way of shortening that costs money or effort · a node goes back only when it can be emptied, so the bill follows nodes and has a floor · more Pods help only when Pods are what's short.

# Examples

All timings below are built from the sourced defaults in `facts.md`; where a step has no source, it says "model" and should be hedged on the page as "typically" or a range. They are ranges for the designer to build a story timeline on, not Fennel's own figures.

### x-01
objective: o-2
example: Where the minutes go when a spike needs new nodes, as a hedged range (minutes after traffic crosses the target). (1) Measurement and decision: the Metrics Server collects every 15 s and the HPA looks every 15 s, so a request for more copies is typically written within about 0.5 to 1 minute (c-01 note, c-08; derived). (2) Pods created and placed: seconds; those that fit on free room start at once, the rest wait `Pending` (c-04). (3) The cluster autoscaler notices within its 10 s scan, simulates a node, and asks the provider for one (c-04). (4) The machine starts and joins: under half a minute on a fast provisioner by AWS's own figure (c-07) to "multiple minutes" with pool-based autoscaling (c-06); AKS Automatic promises 99.9 percent of qualifying Pods ready within 5 minutes (c-07 note). (5) On a node with no copy of the image: image download plus app start, then the readiness check passes (model: tens of seconds to a couple of minutes; Ch 4's and Ch 6's research cover image download and readiness); only then does the Pod join its Service's list (Ch 7's claim). Sum: copies that fit on spare room are serving in about 1 to 3 minutes; copies that needed a new node typically in about 3 to 8 minutes, with fast provisioners at the low end and pool-based autoscaling plus a large image at the high end.
why: Gives the designer a defensible minute-by-minute base case with each step labelled by who owns it (the HPA loop, the scheduler, the autoscaler, the provider, the app). It shows that the HPA's own decision is the quick part, which is why "we scaled but still slowed down" is about the later steps.
source: derived from c-01, c-04, c-06, c-07, c-08 in facts.md; step 5 is model (low confidence)

### x-02
objective: o-2
example: Is "`dispatch` lagged for the first twenty minutes" plausible? Yes, but only at the long end and with a stacked cause; the base case in x-01 is roughly 3 to 8 minutes. Things the sources show can stretch it: a failed or unavailable node request retried after a backoff (GKE: "an initial five-minute backoff" growing to 30 minutes; AKS: up to 30 minutes in backoff; AWS's post: an enterprise customer with 10,000 pending Pods saw 23-minute provisioning delays from repeated capacity-error retries); the autoscaler's simplified simulation needing "multiple iterations" before all waiting Pods are placed; a maximum (copies or pool size) set too low, which leaves Pods `Pending` indefinitely (c-03, c-05); requests that make the new Pods bigger than the machine kind the pool adds (they never fit); a slow-starting app or large image; and the HPA acting on a measurement that lags a spike. For a 20-minute story, use two or three of these in a row (for example: the first node request fails and backs off about five minutes; the retry succeeds; the image is large and the app takes a few minutes to pass readiness), and say so; don't present 20 minutes as what autoscaling normally costs. A corrected, safer headline for the page: "a few minutes when all goes well, much longer when something stacks up".
why: Lets the story keep the entry's "twenty minutes" honestly, by giving it causes, and stops a manager from learning that "autoscaling takes twenty minutes".
source: https://docs.cloud.google.com/kubernetes-engine/docs/concepts/cluster-autoscaler (backoff); https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/aks/cluster-autoscaler-overview (backoff, up to 30 minutes); https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/containers/faster-nodes-smarter-scaling-whats-new-inside-amazon-elastic-kubernetes-service-amazon-eks-auto-mode/ (23 minutes); https://github.com/kubernetes/autoscaler/blob/master/cluster-autoscaler/FAQ.md (simulation); synthesis is model

### x-03
objective: o-2
example: What teams do about lag, each with its cost (for a table on the page). Higher minimum number of copies: the spike meets existing Pods immediately; you pay for those copies all day, and the nodes under them (c-03; derived). Lower target (say 50 percent instead of 70): by the formula in c-01 the HPA asks for more copies sooner and keeps headroom; more machines are running at every moment. Scale on a schedule (copies raised at 16:30 on Fridays) or predictively: the room is ready before the rush; someone has to know the pattern and a wrong guess wastes money or still lags (model; both exist as features or add-ons, not verified here; Ch 13). Spare room kept ready with low-priority placeholder Pods: a real Pod takes the room at once, no wait for a node; "trades cost for scheduling latency" and you pay for the empty room (c-11). Faster-starting Pods (smaller image, quicker app start, a readiness check that passes sooner): shorter step 5 in x-01; costs engineering time, not rent (model). A faster node provisioner: shorter step 4 (c-07); a migration and a tool to run (model).
why: Pairs each remedy with the thing it spends, so the reader can see that every remedy buys time with money or effort, and that none makes the lag disappear.
source: c-01, c-03, c-11 in facts.md; scheduled and predictive scaling and faster-starting Pods are model (low confidence); GKE's "balanced" profile "keeps more resources available for incoming Pods" (https://docs.cloud.google.com/kubernetes-engine/docs/concepts/cluster-autoscaler) is a provider-side version of the same trade

### x-04
objective: o-3
example: The database limit as arithmetic (illustrative numbers, labelled as such). Suppose an app opens 20 connections per copy and its PostgreSQL server allows 100 (the typical default, c-12). Five copies use all 100. An autoscaler that raises copies from 5 to 10 because the app is slow asks for 200 connections: the extra Pods can't get one, and some existing ones fail too. Meanwhile, if the slow part is the app waiting on the database, each Pod's processor use stays low (c-02), so the HPA may not scale at all while requests queue; if it does scale on a rising measurement, the new Pods add load to the one component that is already the limit. The same shape applies to a payment provider's rate limit: more callers, same ceiling.
why: Shows the two failure modes of the predict-then-reveal case (autoscaler does nothing; autoscaler makes it worse) with one number a manager can check against. It makes "what is the bottleneck after the app?" a concrete question to ask.
source: model for the numbers (illustrative); the 100 is c-12; the mechanism is derived from c-01, c-02, c-12

### x-05
objective: o-4
example: Scaling down, as a timeline after the Friday rush ends (hedged, from defaults). Traffic falls around 19:00. The HPA keeps the highest recommendation of the last 5 minutes (c-09), so copies start falling about 19:05 to 19:06. Nodes empty out only then. A node must have its Pods' requests under half its capacity, all Pods movable, and stay that way 10 minutes (c-10), and no node is removed within 10 minutes of the last scale-up; so the first nodes are typically returned about 19:15 to 19:25, one pass at a time, and any node holding a Pod the autoscaler can't move stays. Bill: nodes are rented for the hours they exist, so the saving between the end of the rush and the removal is small; the saving between Saturday morning and the next Friday is large only if the nodes really were removed. A "minimum" of copies sets a floor of nodes (c-03, c-05), and padded requests keep nodes looking "needed" (m-29).
why: Makes "scaling down is slow and cautious on purpose" visible and ties the bill to nodes, not copies.
source: derived from c-09, c-10 in facts.md; times are an illustration built on defaults

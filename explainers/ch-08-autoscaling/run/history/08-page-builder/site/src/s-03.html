§ 03 A loop that rents machines

The HPA’s work ends at the record. When it raises dispatch’s number, the new Pods are placed like any others, by their requests, on whichever nodes have room. On the Friday in Chapter 5 that is exactly where things stopped: the nodes were full on paper, one new Pod had no room, and it waited for an hour until Ari’s team rented two more machines.

Managed Kubernetes offers a loop for that last step as well, usually as a setting that the platform team switches on: the cluster autoscaler, or your provider’s equivalent. It can do the two things that only people at Fennel could do before, which are to ask the provider for another node and to hand one back. Ari’s team switched it on in the same week that dispatch got its HPA.

Answer the question above to see what this loop watches

The cluster autoscaler watches for one thing: Pods that the scheduler has tried to place and couldn’t. It never looks at how busy the nodes are, for the same reason the scheduler doesn’t. Room in a cluster is counted in reservations, as Chapter 5 showed, so a node at 85% is not full as long as its Pods’ requests fit, and a new node would change nothing for Pods that already have one. The guess that it reacts to busy machines isn’t far off, because the first layer does: the HPA starts from a reading of use, and busy Pods are what make it ask for more copies. The second layer reacts only to what the first one leaves behind, which is Pods without a node.

That one trigger explains two earlier evenings. Had this loop been running on the Friday in Chapter 5, the waiting dispatch Pod would have been given a new node without anyone on Ari’s team having to notice it and rent one. But it would not have replaced node 03 when that machine died in Chapter 4, because every Pod from node 03 found room on the three nodes that were left, and so nothing was waiting.

Before it asks for a node, the loop works out whether a new one would actually give the waiting Pods room, which means it has to know what a new node would be: how much memory, how many cores. That is the job of a node pool, a group of identical machines that the cluster can add to or shrink. Fennel has one, made of the 16 GB, 4-core machines it has rented all along, and Ari’s team gave it a minimum of 3 nodes and a maximum of 8. The loop moves the pool between those two numbers and never past them. The maximum in particular is a person’s decision, and it works as a cap on spending: when the pool reaches it, the loop stops asking, and any Pod that still has no room simply waits.

New term · 02 / 03

cluster autoscaler

A loop that asks the provider for another node when a Pod has no room, and hands a node back when its Pods all fit elsewhere.

The loop Chapter 5 was missing.

New term · 03 / 03

node pool

A group of identical machines in a cluster, with a smallest and a largest size that people set; nodes are added to it and removed from it.

Some providers say node group.

A cluster can have several pools, one for each kind of machine, and the two dashed pools in the figure are there to show why it might. If orders ever outgrew the 13 GB that a general node can give its Pods, no number of general nodes would help, as Chapter 5 found, and the loop could add nothing useful until someone had defined a pool of bigger machines. Ari has also proposed a pool of spot machines, which cost less because the provider can take one back at short notice (AWS, for one, gives two minutes’ warning, as of November 2026). A service that can be interrupted, such as notifications, could live there. orders and payments would not.

Through the loop lens, the cluster autoscaler wants every Pod on a node, on no more nodes than that takes. It sees the Pods the scheduler couldn’t place, and how much of each node is reserved. When those differ from what it wants, it adds a node from a pool, up to the pool’s maximum, or it empties a node it can do without and gives it back. How carefully it does the second of those comes later in the chapter.

My engineers say Karpenter, or node auto-provisioning. Is that this loop?

It is the same job done by a different tool, and the names differ by provider. Some newer tools, such as Karpenter and the “node auto-provisioning” or fully managed modes that several providers build on it or model on it (names you may hear include Autopilot and Auto Mode), skip the pre-made pools. They pick a machine to fit the Pods that are waiting, within limits the team sets, and they tend to start nodes faster. What they react to is the same: Pods with no room.

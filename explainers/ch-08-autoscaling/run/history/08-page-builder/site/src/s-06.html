§ 06 Scaling down, and Morgan’s bill

The way down is slower than the way up, and it is slower on purpose. On the Friday of the push, orders began to ease at about seven. The HPA saw the reading fall straight away, but by default it lowers the number of copies only as far as the highest number it wanted at any moment in the last five minutes, so that a brief dip in orders doesn’t remove copies the next minute will need. dispatch started to come down at about 19:05 and was back at three copies by about 19:20.

Nodes come down more slowly still, because removing Pods doesn’t remove a node. The cluster autoscaler gives a node back only when three things hold, by default: the Pods on it reserve less than half of its room, every one of those Pods would fit on some other node, and both have stayed true for about ten minutes.

Node 07 went back to the provider at about 19:30, after two hours on the bill. Node 06 stayed, for the same reason it stayed in the week the loops were switched on: its one Pod reserves 2 GB, and no other node has more than 1 GB free. Had there been room elsewhere, the loop would have emptied node 06 in the way Kubernetes always removes a Pod from a node, by deleting it and letting a replacement be placed on another node, as in Chapter 4. Notice, too, that the test is about reservations again and not about use, as it was when this loop adds a node.

None of this was quite what Morgan had expected from autoscaling.

Fennel went from five nodes to four, which is about a fifth fewer node-hours in a week, against work that falls by two-thirds on a quiet day. The copies do follow the work closely, but Fennel doesn’t pay for copies. It pays for nodes, and nodes come and go as whole machines. Underneath them sits a floor that no amount of quiet will lower: the minimums that people chose, the services that have no autoscaler at all, and every request, because a node full of padded reservations never looks empty to this loop. That is Chapter 5’s lesson about padding, with a second cost attached. A cluster with many more nodes than Fennel’s typically moves in finer steps, but its floor is made of the same things.

What else stops a node being given back?

Several things, with default settings. A Pod that no loop would replace keeps its node, since removing it would lose it for good. So can a Pod that stores files on the node’s own disk, and so can a team’s rule limiting how many copies of a service may be down at once (these are called disruption budgets, and Chapter 14 comes back to them). Timing matters too: by default, no node is given back within about ten minutes of one being added.

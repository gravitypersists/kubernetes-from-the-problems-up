## Objectives

- o-1: after reading, the reader can explain the two layers of autoscaling as two loops and how they chain: one loop reads a measurement of a service's Pods and changes the number of copies wanted in the record (the HorizontalPodAutoscaler); the new Pods are placed by requests like any others; when they don't fit they wait, and a second loop (the cluster autoscaler, or the provider's equivalent) sees the waiting Pods and rents a node, later giving back nodes it can empty; and can fill in the loop lens for both.
- o-2: after reading, the reader can predict where the minutes go when traffic rises faster than the cluster can follow (the measurement's delay, starting a Pod, renting and starting a node, readiness before a Pod joins its Service's list), say what customers see meanwhile, and name what teams do about it (a higher minimum, scaling earlier or on a schedule, spare room kept ready, faster-starting Pods), each with its cost.
- o-3: after reading, the reader can explain why adding Pods doesn't help when the limit is somewhere else (a database, the payment provider, a few long-lived connections), predict what autoscaling does in that case, and ask what the bottleneck is after the app.
- o-4: after reading, the reader can say what scaling down does and doesn't do to the bill (Pods are removed cautiously and nodes are given back only once they can be emptied; the minimum and the requests set the floor), and what a node pool is and why the loop that adds nodes needs them: which kind of machine to add, and which services belong on machines that are cheaper but can be taken back.

## Research questions

- q-1 (o-1): How the HorizontalPodAutoscaler works, at a manager's level: what it watches by default and commonly (average processor use **as a share of each Pod's request**, so requests matter again), the target, the calculation in one line, minimum and maximum, how often it looks (default sync period), its tolerance, what it writes (the Deployment's number of copies) and what it needs to work (requests set; a source of measurements). What happens at the maximum? What does it do with Pods that aren't ready yet? Status of the API as of 2026 (autoscaling/v2).
- q-2 (o-1, o-4): How nodes get added and removed: the cluster autoscaler (triggered by Pods that can't be scheduled, not by busy nodes; it simulates whether a new node from a pool would fit them; scale-down of nodes that are underused and whose Pods fit elsewhere, with its default waits and what blocks it); what the big managed providers offer as of 2026 and what they call it (GKE cluster autoscaler / node auto-provisioning / Autopilot, EKS with Cluster Autoscaler, Karpenter or Auto Mode, AKS cluster autoscaler / node auto-provisioning), enough to speak generically and hedge; which behaviours differ between the cluster autoscaler and Karpenter-style provisioners. Node pools (node groups): what one is, per-pool minimum and maximum, how the autoscaler picks a pool; spot / preemptible pools (discount range from one provider's page with a date, notice period before a machine is taken back). Ch 5's facts c-24, c-25 may be reused by reference.
- q-3 (o-2): Where does the time go in a scale-up, with sourced typical figures and defaults: metrics delay and the HPA's period; Pod start (scheduling, image download, app start, readiness check); a new node (provider's machine start plus joining the cluster: documented or measured ranges for at least one managed provider, hedged); the HPA's scale-down stabilization window (default 5 minutes) and scale-up behaviour; the cluster autoscaler's scale-down delays (defaults around 10 minutes). Is "`dispatch` lagged for the first twenty minutes" plausible for a late scale-up that needed new nodes, and what would a realistic minute-by-minute timeline be? What do teams do about lag (higher minimum, scheduled or predictive scaling, spare capacity kept ready by low-priority placeholder Pods, smaller images, lower target), each with its cost?
- q-4 (o-3): When more Pods don't help: a shared database or connection limit, a downstream provider's rate limit, a slow dependency (what happens to the chosen metric: processor use may stay low while requests queue, so the autoscaler doesn't add Pods; or it adds Pods that make the database's load worse, e.g. exhausting database connections). - q-5 (o-4): Scaling down and cost: how cautiously the HPA removes Pods (stabilization) and why (avoiding flapping); when a node can be given back and what stops it; that the bill follows nodes (standard mode), so it tracks traffic only as far as nodes are actually removed; how much teams typically save or how over-provisioned clusters typically are (one or two recent sourced figures; Ch 5's Datadog and CNCF figures may be reused by reference).

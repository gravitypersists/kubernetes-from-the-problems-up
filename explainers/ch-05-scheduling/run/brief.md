## Objectives

- o-1: after reading, the reader can explain that the scheduler places each Pod by the room it *reserves* (its requests, counted separately for memory and processor), not by what it actually uses, and therefore that requests, packed onto nodes, decide how many machines Fennel pays for in the provider's standard mode (and what it pays for in per-Pod-billed modes); including why free room split across nodes can't hold one large Pod (m-26), and what node pools add.
- o-2: after reading, the reader can predict the effect of over-requesting (more nodes, idle reserved room, Pods left waiting: m-07, m-08) and of under-requesting or setting limits too low (crowded nodes, a slowed processor, memory kills, evictions) on cost and reliability, and say why some unused room is deliberate headroom (insurance for a node failure like 03:04, with the cluster now on three nodes).
- o-3: after reading, the reader can interpret "pending" and "OOM-killed" (and an eviction) as symptoms, name which loop is involved using the loop lens, and ask the questions they raise ("Is that a leak or real growth? What does it do to our node count?").

## Research questions

- q-1 (o-1): How does the default scheduler choose a node (filtering out nodes that can't fit, then scoring the rest), and what role do requests play? Does it ever look at actual usage? What is a node's allocatable room vs. its full capacity (room kept back for the system)? Is spreading a service's copies across nodes a default behaviour, and what single sentence covers affinity/taints accurately? State it at a manager's level without plugin names.
- q-2 (o-1): How do requests translate into cost on managed Kubernetes? In standard modes (EKS, GKE Standard, AKS) the customer pays for nodes; in per-Pod-billed modes (e.g. GKE Autopilot, EKS on Fargate, AKS Automatic, verify which exist and how they bill) what is billed: requests or usage? Keep providers generic in the text; facts here are for accuracy. Is there credible, sourced data on typical cluster utilization or over-requesting (e.g. CNCF or vendor reports) suitable for a manager, clearly labelled as one survey's figure?
- q-3 (o-2): Requests vs. limits for memory and processor: what happens when a container exceeds its memory limit (OOM-killed and restarted), when it exceeds its processor limit (throttled, not killed), when no limit is set, and when a node runs short of memory (node-pressure eviction: which Pods go first, at a manager's level, e.g. Pods using more than they requested; QoS classes only if needed)? What are the defaults when requests or limits are left unset? Current docs.
- q-4 (o-2): What do managers and newcomers get wrong about resource settings? Evidence for m-07 ("setting resources high is the safe choice"), m-08 ("Kubernetes saves money automatically"), m-26 ("enough free room in total means it fits"); anything new (`local-NN`). Why does a bill often go *up* after a move to Kubernetes (over-requesting, headroom, system overhead per node, a control-plane fee: name only), sourced where possible?
- q-5 (o-3): What does "pending" mean precisely, what causes a Pod to stay pending for lack of room, and how does the reason surface (the scheduler's recorded message, e.g. "0/3 nodes are available: insufficient memory")? Does the scheduler keep retrying? What changes when a node is added (preview only: the cluster autoscaler is Ch 8)?
- q-6 (o-3): How does an OOM-kill show up to a team (the "OOMKilled" reason, exit code 137, restart count, possible crash-loop back-off, which Ch 4 introduced), and how do engineers tell a memory leak from real growth at a manager's level (memory climbing steadily regardless of traffic vs. tracking load)? How do teams pick requests in practice (from observed usage; tools named only)?
- q-7 (o-1): What is a node pool on the managed services (a group of identical machines; several pools with different machine types, e.g. memory-heavy, GPU, cheaper interruptible "spot" machines), and what single idea should a manager take away? Keep to what this chapter needs.

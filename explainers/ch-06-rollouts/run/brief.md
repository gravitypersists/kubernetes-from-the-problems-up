## Objectives

- o-1: after reading, the reader can explain how a rolling update replaces one version of a service with the next without downtime: the Deployment, a loop that manages versions by managing ReplicaSets, brings up new Pods a few at a time and removes old ones only as new ones become ready; it needs spare room while both run (Ch 5), and it replaces Sam's by-hand Tuesday from Ch 3 and the "Nobody deploy anything" message.
- o-2: after reading, the reader can predict which bad releases health checks stop and which they don't: a version that crashes or never becomes ready stalls the rollout with old Pods still serving, while a version that is healthy but wrong (the `catalog` $0 prices) rolls out completely (m-09); and can tell a readiness probe ("ready for traffic") from a liveness probe ("still alive, or restart it").
- o-3: after reading, the reader can explain rollback in terms of desired state (declaring the old version again, which the same loop rolls out the same way, using the old ReplicaSet it kept), and what rollback assumes: that the old image still exists and that the version label points where you think (tags vs. digests); and that it doesn't undo changes outside the Pods, such as data written by the bad version.

## Research questions

- q-1 (o-1): How does a Deployment perform a rolling update by default: it creates a new ReplicaSet and scales new up and old down; what `maxSurge` and `maxUnavailable` default to (25% each, rounded how?) and what that means for a 3- or 4-copy service; does an old Pod get removed only once a new one is ready? What does it need in spare room, and what happens if a surge Pod can't be placed (pending)? What triggers a rollout (a change to the Pod template, e.g. the image) and what doesn't (changing replicas)?
- q-2 (o-1): Where does a new version come from at a manager's level (CI builds and pushes an image, then the Deployment's image field is changed, by a person, a pipeline or a GitOps tool), kept light; what do teams that "ship several times a day" typically do differently from a Thursday-night release?
- q-3 (o-2): Readiness vs. liveness (and startup) probes: what each checks, what Kubernetes does when each fails (readiness: removed from traffic, not restarted; liveness: container restarted), what happens with no probes at all (a Pod counts as ready once its containers start), and the common mistakes (liveness checks that depend on a database, probes that test only "the process answers"). What does a rollout of a crashing version look like (new Pods crash-loop or never become ready; the rollout stops progressing; `progressDeadlineSeconds` default and what "failed to progress" does and does not do: no automatic rollback)?
- q-4 (o-2): What do managers and newcomers get wrong about rollouts and health checks? Evidence for m-09 ("rolling updates guarantee a safe release"); anything new (`local-NN`), e.g. "a failed rollout rolls itself back", "health checks test whether the app is correct". Is there a credible, sourced example or statistic about changes causing incidents (e.g. a well-known report that most outages follow a change), labelled as one source's figure?
- q-5 (o-3): Rollback: what `kubectl rollout undo` does in desired-state terms (the Deployment's template set back to an earlier revision; the kept old ReplicaSet scaled up again), the revision history limit default, and whether rolling back is itself a rolling update. Is undoing via the command different from changing the manifest back in version control (drift between the record and the files)?
- q-6 (o-3): Tags vs. digests: can a tag like `latest` or `v13` be moved to a different image; does a digest pin one exact image; what happens on rollback if the old image was deleted from the registry or the tag moved; what practices teams use (immutable tags, pinning digests), kept at a manager's level. What does rollback not undo (data written, messages sent, schema changes), with Ch 10 as the forward pointer?
- q-7 (o-1, o-2): Canary and blue-green releases: one accurate sentence each, and whether plain Kubernetes Deployments do them by themselves or need extra tools; enough to name them correctly.

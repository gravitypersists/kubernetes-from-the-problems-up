Say what you want, not what to do: desired state and the reconcile loop

A year on, Fennel runs on managed Kubernetes. The same machine dies at 3 a.m., and this time nobody wakes up. This chapter shows how: a written record of what Fennel wants running, and many small loops that keep the cluster matching it. It also shows what those loops can’t do.

It’s Year 3. Fennel now runs on managed Kubernetes from its cloud provider. The provider runs Kubernetes’ own machinery, and Fennel’s services run on four machines, numbered 01 to 04 as before. Kubernetes calls each machine in a cluster a node. Ari, the platform lead Fennel hired this year, led the move.

One Saturday at 03:04, machine 03 dies again. It’s node 03 now, and it has orders and a dispatch copy on it, just as it did a year ago. Nobody’s phone rings. Sam sleeps through it and reads about it over breakfast.

On Monday, Jordan asks Ari how Kubernetes knew what to do.

“Nobody told it what to do. We told it what we want.”

Ari

The rest of this chapter is what Ari’s sentence means, and what it doesn’t promise.

§ 02 Say what you want, not what to do

There are two ways to write down what Fennel needs when a machine dies. Fig. 01 writes down the same night both ways: as the task list Sam worked through after 03:04 last year, and as a goal, which says how many copies of each service should be running.

COMPARECheck 02 / 09

This time it’s node 04 that dies at 3 a.m., not node 03. Which of the two documents in Fig. 01 still says the right thing to do, unchanged?

Sam’s list says what to do, step by step. Engineers call that style imperative. Fennel’s goal says what should be running and leaves the steps to the system. That style is declarative. What a declarative goal describes is the cluster’s desired state.

New term · 01 / 04

desired state

What you want running, written down: “storefront: 3 copies”. Kubernetes keeps working to make the cluster match it.

declarative: say what you want · imperative: say what to do

You met it in Fig. 01: Fennel’s goal.

The difference shows on a night nobody pictured. A task list written for another night is simply wrong. A goal still says what’s missing, so whatever acts on the goal can work out the steps for itself.

ANALOGYYou know this from managing people. A task list (“call the supplier, then rebook the van”) works until reality differs from what you pictured. A goal (“every order out by 6 p.m.”) handed to a good owner keeps working: the owner watches one number, notices when it slips and fixes it, without being told how.

WHERE THIS ANALOGY BREAKSThe owners inside Kubernetes have no judgement. They don’t ask why copies keep dying. They don’t push back when a goal can’t be met: with no room anywhere, a new copy just waits. They don’t escalate, and they will restart a broken app forever. And they act only on the latest goal, not on the history of instructions. In a person, all that would be a problem. In a machine, it means you always know what it will do next: close the gap between the goal and what’s running.

What if the goal changes twice in a minute?

Say someone changes storefront’s goal from 2 copies to 5 and then, a minute later, to 3. Kubernetes doesn’t have to pass through 5 first. Whenever it looks, it compares the latest goal with what’s running and heads for that, so it ends at 3. There’s no queue of orders to fall behind on. Engineers call this behaviour “level-triggered”.

Fennel writes its goals in files called manifests. Fig. 02 is the manifest for storefront, shortened. Its line replicas: 3 is the desired state: three copies. Teams keep manifests in version control, so every change to a goal is reviewed before it goes in, and leaves a history.

Chapter 3 ended on a question: what if the record were the thing the machines act on, instead of a note about them? With Kubernetes, a written goal like Fig. 02 is that record.

§ 03 Pods: what Kubernetes counts

One more word before Ari applies the storefront manifest. Kubernetes doesn’t count or place containers directly. It wraps each copy’s container in a Pod, and it’s Pods that Kubernetes counts, places and replaces. Most Pods hold just one container, so in conversation people often use “Pod” and “container” for the same thing. Sometimes a small helper container rides along in the same Pod.

New term · 02 / 04

Pod

The wrapper Kubernetes puts around one container, or a few that must run together. It counts, places and replaces Pods.

Chapter 2’s container, wrapped. Named as in a pod of whales, or a pea pod.

Each loop on moving day did the same three things. It looked at the record and at the cluster, compared what’s wanted with what’s there, and acted to close the gap. Then it went back to looking. That pattern is a reconcile loop. A controller is a part of Kubernetes that runs one, for one kind of thing: the ReplicaSet loop is the controller for ReplicaSets. One pass of a reconcile loop is called “reconciling”. The scheduler and the node agent work the same way, though engineers don’t usually call them controllers.

New term · 03 / 04

reconcile loop · controller

A loop that compares what the record says is wanted with what it observes, acts to close the gap, and does it again, forever. A controller runs one, for one kind of thing.

which loop · watching what · doing what

You met three on moving day.

Those three blanks are a question you can ask whenever Kubernetes does something: which loop, watching what, doing what? Each step of moving day answered it. At the fourth step, for example, the loop was the scheduler, watching for Pods with no node, and writing a node into each one. Kubernetes is built from many loops like these, so the same question will explain each new part of Kubernetes in the chapters ahead.

ANALOGYThe name fits. Kubernetes is Greek for helmsman, or pilot. A helmsman doesn’t plot every wave. They hold a heading, and correct the wheel whenever the ship drifts off it. (Engineers shorten the name to K8s: a K, the eight letters in between, then an s.)

WHERE THIS ANALOGY BREAKSA ship has one helmsman. A cluster has many loops, each holding its own small heading, and no captain giving them orders. And a helmsman reads the weather; a loop only compares what’s wanted with what’s there.

Chapter 1 left Jordan with a question to ask about any machine Fennel depends on:

“If this box vanished tonight, how long until we’re back, and who has to be awake?”

Chapter 1

Year 3’s answer: with default settings, roughly six or seven minutes, sometimes less; and nobody.

Those minutes still cost something. orders was down for all of them, because Fennel runs it as a single copy. When its one Pod stopped, no other copy could take orders while the loops replaced it. Running a service as one copy is what Chapter 1 called a single point of failure. The loops restore what’s declared; they don’t prevent the gap. So here is a question worth asking Ari: “Which of our services run as a single copy?”

Can it be faster than six minutes?

It can be. In a cloud, Kubernetes asks the provider whether a silent machine still exists. If the provider says it’s gone, its Pods are cleaned up without the full five-minute wait. Engineers can also shorten or lengthen the waits, app by app. Fennel’s numbers here are the defaults. The wait exists because, at first, a network glitch looks the same as a dead machine, and replacing too eagerly could leave two copies running where one was wanted.

What about machine 03 itself?

Kubernetes replaced the Pods, not the machine. Until a machine is added back, the cluster has three nodes and less spare room. Chapter 5 is about that room.

EXPLAINCheck 05 / 09

Jordan asks Ari: “When node 03 died, what worked out that orders should go to node 04?” Which answer is accurate?

No part of Kubernetes made a plan for 03:04. The node loop didn’t know what a ReplicaSet was. The ReplicaSet loop didn’t know why its Pods had gone. The scheduler didn’t know there had been a failure. Each one read the record, did its one job and wrote the result back, and the next loop picked the work up from there. That’s how the work is split, with no single conductor.

Chapter 3 called Kubernetes an orchestrator, for the jobs it does, and it is one. Kubernetes’ own documentation adds a warning about what the word suggests. It defines orchestration as following a fixed script, “first do A, then B, then C”. Kubernetes, it says, instead “comprises a set of independent, composable control processes”. And: “Centralized control is also not required.” Chapter 3 used the word for what gets done; the documentation is talking about how it gets done.

There’s a practical reason for building it this way. The same documentation prefers “simple controllers rather than one, monolithic set of control loops”, because controllers can fail. With many separate loops, one can fail without stopping the rest.

But engineers talk about “the controller manager”

They do. Kubernetes’ built-in controllers ship together as one program, to keep things simple to run. Inside it, each controller is still a separate loop watching its own kind of thing, like separate teams sharing one office building. The scheduler and the node agents are separate programs again.

None of 03:04 needed a release, or anyone applying anything. The loops had been running all along.

§ 06 Replaced, not repaired

PREDICTCheck 06 / 09

Since 03:10, an orders Pod has been running on node 04. Is it the Pod that was on node 03?

Ari’s dashboard makes the difference visible. On Friday it listed orders-5c8x on node 03. Since 03:10 it has listed orders-q2rw on node 04. A Pod is never moved. It’s replaced by a new one, made from the same template, with a new name, a new address and nothing from inside the old one.

That’s why Kubernetes treats Pods as disposable. People rarely create Pods by hand: a controller makes them, and each one lasts until it finishes its work, is removed, or its node fails. The new address is also why storefront needed another loop, Chapter 7’s, to find the new orders Pod.

What “disposable” asks of the app

Engineers will recognise this from the twelve-factor app, a widely used checklist for building software. Its word is “disposability”: an app’s processes “can be started or stopped at a moment’s notice”. For a service running on Kubernetes, that comes down to four things:

  • Keep nothing important inside the Pod. Fennel’s orders live in its database, outside the app, as Chapter 2 said; where that database should run is Chapter 10.
  • Survive being stopped or replaced at any moment.
  • Start in seconds, not minutes. A slow start adds to every replacement’s gap.
  • Finish in-flight work when asked to stop. By default, Kubernetes gives a Pod 30 seconds to do that.

These are the app’s job, not Kubernetes’. They’re the questions to ask whenever one of your services moves onto the cluster.

Self-healing, honestly

You’ll hear what happened at 03:04 called self-healing. After last year’s 3 a.m. pages, it’s easy to hear that as “Kubernetes makes our app reliable”. What the loops actually do is restore the declared state: they restart containers that crash, and replace the Pods of nodes that die. Here is what they can’t do:

  • Fix a bug. An app that crashes as it starts is restarted, crashes again, and is restarted again, forever. §08 shows one.
  • Find room that isn’t there. If no node has room for a new Pod, the Pod waits, unplaced, until room appears. Chapter 5 gives this a name.
  • Bring back what was inside a Pod. Anything written inside a Pod is gone with it. Keeping data safe is Chapter 10.
  • Keep a single copy up. As at 03:04, a service with one copy is down while that copy is replaced.

Kubernetes heals the count of what’s running. Whether the app itself is healthy is still your engineers’ work.

§ 07 Who runs what

You’ve now met every part of a cluster that a manager needs by name. Fig. 05 puts them in one place, with who runs each part.

The record and the loops that work on it, apart from the node agents, make up the control plane. In front of the record sits the API server, the front door you met on moving day. Everything that reads or writes the record goes through it: people applying manifests, and every loop alike. Engineers call the node agent on each node the kubelet. It starts what’s been assigned to its node, and reports back.

New term · 04 / 04

control plane

The cluster’s record and the loops that work on it, behind the API server. On a managed service, your provider runs it.

You met each part in §04 and §05.

On a managed service, the provider runs the whole control plane, in every mode, and upgrades it to new Kubernetes versions. Who looks after the nodes depends on the mode. In each big provider’s standard mode, the customer does; at Fennel, that’s Ari’s team. In the fully managed modes, the provider does. Either way, Fennel pays for the machines its Pods run on. And some things are always Fennel’s: what goes into the record, its images and code, its data, who may change what, and watching and answering alerts.

Without a managed service, Fennel’s own team would run the control plane too, including keeping the record safe.

What is the cluster’s record, exactly?

Engineers often call it etcd, after the software usually used to store it; some providers store it differently. Only the API server reads and writes it, and every loop goes through the API server.

What if the control plane itself goes down?

Pods that are running keep running, but no Pod is replaced or placed until the control plane is back: each part keeps doing what it was last told. What providers promise about this is Chapter 14.

ANSWER“So Kubernetes is doing its job. What’s wrong with the app? And is restarting hiding the problem from us?”

The loop compares one thing, a running container, and acts on it; it can’t see the missing setting, so restarting is all it can do. And a service that restarts quietly can look “handled” on a dashboard while texts and emails stop going out. Chapter 12 comes back to how teams catch that.

If your question was “can Kubernetes fix it?”, that’s the trap this chapter is about: the loops restore the count; they don’t repair the app.

In short

  • With Kubernetes you write down what you want, the desired state, in manifests. Applying one records the intention; it doesn’t run anything.
  • Many small loops each compare one part of the record with what they observe, and act to close the gap, forever. No part plans for the others; work passes between them through the record.
  • Kubernetes runs Pods, and Pods are disposable: a dead node’s Pods are replaced by new ones, in minutes with default settings, with nobody awake. The app has to tolerate that, and the loops can’t fix a broken app.
  • Your provider runs the control plane. What goes into the record, and whether your app survives being replaced, stays yours.

Sam’s list says what to do, step by step: imperative. Fennel’s goal says what should be running and leaves the steps to the system: declarative. What a declarative goal describes is the cluster’s desired state.

NEW TERM · 01 / 04
desired state
What you want running, written down: “storefront: 3 copies”. Kubernetes keeps working to make the cluster match it.
declarative: say what you want · imperative: say what to do
You met it in Fig. 01: Fennel’s goal.
ANALOGYYou know this from managing people. A task list (“call the supplier, then rebook the van”) works until reality differs from what you pictured. A goal (“every order out by 6 p.m.”) handed to a good owner keeps working: the owner watches one number, notices when it slips and fixes it, without being told how. WHERE THIS ANALOGY BREAKSKubernetes’ owners have no judgement. They don’t ask why copies keep dying, don’t push back when a goal can’t be met (with no room anywhere, a new copy just waits), don’t escalate, and will restart a broken app forever. They act only on the latest goal, not on the history of instructions. In a person that would be a problem; in a machine it’s the point.

▸ D2 · What if the goal changes twice in a minute? (collapsed) From 2 copies to 5 and then to 3: Kubernetes heads straight for 3; there’s no queue of orders to fall behind on. Engineers call this “level-triggered”.

Fennel’s goal is written in files called manifests (dfn). Fig. 02 is storefront’s, shortened; replicas: 3 is the desired state. Teams keep manifests in version control, so every change to the goal is reviewed before it goes in and leaves a history.

Chapter 3 ended on a question: what if the record were the thing the machines act on, instead of a note about them? With Kubernetes, it is. (What “apply” does is left for §04.)

s-03 · § 03

Pods: what Kubernetes counts

name → small figure → term cardo-3~1.0 min

Introduce the Pod as the unit Kubernetes counts, places and replaces, just before lane A needs it. (Disposability waits for §06.)

One more word before Ari applies the manifest. Kubernetes doesn’t count or place containers directly. It wraps each copy’s container in a Pod, and counts, places and replaces Pods. Most Pods hold one container, so people often say “Pod” and “container” for the same thing. Sometimes a small helper container rides along; the containers in a Pod always run together on one node and share one address.

Each of those loops did the same three things: looked at the record and the cluster, compared what’s wanted with what’s there, and acted to close the gap. Then it went back to looking. That’s a reconcile loop; a controller is a part of Kubernetes that runs one, for one kind of thing. (The scheduler and the node agent work the same way, though engineers don’t usually call them controllers.)

NEW TERM · 03 / 04 · payoff variant (blueprint ground)
reconcile loop · controller
A loop that compares what the record says is wanted with what it observes, acts to close the gap, and does it again, forever. A controller runs one, for one kind of thing.
which loop · watching what · doing what
You met three on moving day.

The loop lens. From here on, whenever Kubernetes does something, ask three things: which loop, watching what, doing what? The lines on the drawing in §04 already used them. Every feature in later chapters is another loop.

ANALOGYThe name fits. Kubernetes is Greek for helmsman or pilot. A helmsman doesn’t plot every wave: they hold a heading and correct the wheel whenever the ship drifts. (Engineers write K8s: K, the eight letters in between, s.) WHERE THIS ANALOGY BREAKSA ship has one helmsman. A cluster has many loops, each holding its own small heading, and no captain giving them orders. And a helmsman reads the weather; a loop only compares two numbers.

But orders was down for those minutes, because it runs as a single copy: Chapter 1’s single point of failure. The loops restore what’s declared; they don’t prevent the gap. A question worth asking Ari: “Which of our services run as a single copy?”

▸ D3 · Can it be faster than six minutes? It can be: in a cloud, Kubernetes asks the provider whether a silent machine still exists; if it’s gone, its Pods are cleaned up without the full wait. Engineers can also change the waits per service. The wait exists because a glitch looks like a dead machine, and replacing too eagerly would run two copies where one was wanted.

▸ D4 · What about machine 03 itself? Kubernetes replaced the Pods, not the machine. Until a machine is added back, the cluster has three nodes and less spare room. Chapter 5 is about that room.

EXPLAIN · Check 05 / 09 · k-05
Jordan asks Ari: “When node 03 died, what worked out that orders should go to node 04?” Which answer is accurate?
  1. “The control plane did: it saw node 03 die, made a recovery plan, and told each node what to start.”
  2. “The scheduler did. It only finds nodes for Pods that don’t have one; it saw a new orders Pod with no node and picked one with room.” ✓
  3. “Node 04’s agent did: it noticed it had spare room and took over the orders Pod from node 03.”

A (m-03): “No part made a plan. The node loop marked node 03 and removed its Pods; the ReplicaSet loop saw a gap…; the scheduler saw a Pod with no node… Each did its own step, passing work on through the record.” B: “Right. The scheduler didn’t plan a recovery or even need to know there had been a failure…” C: “Node agents start only Pods already assigned to their node; they don’t take work. And nothing was taken over from node 03; the next section says why.”

No single conductor. No part of Kubernetes made a plan for 03:04. The node loop didn’t know what a ReplicaSet was; the ReplicaSet loop didn’t know why its Pods had gone; the scheduler didn’t know there had been a failure. Each read the record, did its one job and wrote the result back, and the next loop picked it up from there.

Chapter 3 called Kubernetes an orchestrator, for the jobs it does, and it is one. Kubernetes’ own documentation adds a warning about the word: it isn’t a conductor working through a score (“first do A, then B, then C”); it’s “a set of independent, composable control processes”. “Centralized control is also not required.” Simple loops rather than one big program also mean one can fail without stopping the rest.

None of this needed a release, or anyone applying anything: the loops were running all along.

▸ D5 · But engineers talk about “the controller manager” (collapsed) They do: the built-in controllers ship as one program, to keep things simple to run. Inside it, each is still a separate loop watching its own kind of thing, like teams sharing an office building. The scheduler and node agents are separate programs again.

s-06 · § 06

Replaced, not repaired

predict (m-06) → requirements → misconception (m-05)o-3~2.25 min

A replacement is a new Pod; say what “disposable” asks of the app; list what the loops can’t fix.

PREDICT · Check 06 / 09 · k-06
Since 03:10, an orders Pod has been running on node 04. Is it the Pod that was on node 03?
  1. No: it’s a new Pod made from the same template, with a new name and a new address. Anything kept inside the old one is gone. ✓
  2. Yes: Kubernetes moved it to node 04, keeping its name and address, so nothing that calls it has to change.

A: “Right. Kubernetes never moves a Pod. It replaces it with a new one, wherever there’s room…” B (m-06): “Virtual machines can be moved like that; Pods can’t. The old Pod went with node 03, and the ReplicaSet loop wrote a new one, which got a new name and a new address…”

Ari’s dashboard showed orders-5c8x on node 03 on Friday, and orders-q2rw on node 04 since 03:10. A Pod is never moved; it’s replaced by a new one with a new name, a new address, and nothing from inside the old one. That’s why Kubernetes treats Pods as disposable: people rarely create them by hand; a controller makes them, and each lasts until it’s finished, removed, or its node fails.

What “disposable” asks of the app (the twelve-factor app’s checklist, which engineers will recognise):

  • keep nothing important inside the Pod (Fennel’s orders live in its database, outside the app; where that database runs is Chapter 10);
  • survive being stopped or replaced at any moment;
  • start in seconds, not minutes: start time adds to every replacement’s gap;
  • finish in-flight work when asked to stop (Kubernetes gives a Pod 30 seconds by default).

These are the app’s job, not Kubernetes’: the questions to ask when a service moves onto the cluster.

Self-healing, honestly. Vendors call what you watched at 03:04 self-healing (dfn), and after last year’s 3 a.m. pages it’s easy to hear “Kubernetes makes our app reliable”. The loops restore the declared state: they restart crashed containers and replace Pods from dead nodes. What they can’t do:

  • fix a bug: an app that crashes on start is restarted, and crashes again, forever (§08);
  • find room that isn’t there: with no node that has room, a new Pod waits, unplaced, until room appears (Chapter 5 names this);
  • bring back what was inside a Pod: anything written there is gone with it (Chapter 10);
  • and from 03:04: a single copy is down while it’s replaced.

Kubernetes heals the count. Whether the app is healthy is still your engineers’ work.

s-07 · § 07

Who runs what

structure figure → term card → applyo-4~2.0 min

Assemble the parts already met into a sketch of the cluster, name the control plane, and say what a managed provider runs and what stays Fennel’s.

You’ve now met every part of a cluster a manager needs by name (Fig. 05).

The record and the loops that work on it, apart from the node agents, are the control plane. In front of the record sits the API server (dfn): everything that reads or writes the record goes through it, people applying manifests and every loop alike. Engineers call the node agent on each node the kubelet.

NEW TERM · 04 / 04
control plane
The cluster’s record and the loops that work on it, behind the API server. On a managed service, your provider runs it.
You met each part in §04 and §05.

On a managed service the provider runs the whole control plane in every mode, and upgrades its Kubernetes version. Who looks after the nodes depends on the mode: in each big provider’s standard mode the customer does (at Fennel, Ari’s team); in fully managed modes the provider does. Either way Fennel pays for the machines its Pods run on, and always owns what goes into the record, its images and code, its data, who may change what, and watching and answering alerts. Without a managed service, Fennel’s team would run the control plane too, and keep the record safe.

▸ D6 · What is the cluster’s record, exactly? Engineers often call it etcd, after the software usually used; some providers store it differently. Only the API server reads and writes it.

▸ D7 · What if the control plane itself goes down? Running Pods keep running, but no Pod is replaced or placed until it’s back: each part keeps doing what it was last told. What providers promise about this is Chapter 14.

The conversation

predict (m-05) → think-then-reveal (apply) → recap → bridgeo-2 o-3~2.25 min

Fennel’s newest service, notifications, sends order texts and emails. On Wednesday a setting it needs goes missing, and every time its container starts, it crashes within seconds.

PREDICT · Check 08 / 09 · k-08
Every time a notifications container starts, it crashes within seconds, because a setting it needs is missing. With default settings, what does Kubernetes do over the next hour?
  1. Restarts it in place again and again, waiting longer between tries, and never stops. ✓
  2. Restarts it a few times, then stops and marks notifications as failed, so someone notices.
  3. Replaces its Pod on a different node each time, in case the node is what’s broken.

A: “Right. The node agent restarts it after about 10 seconds, then 20, 40 and so on, up to 5 minutes between tries: roughly 15 restarts in the first hour, then about 12 an hour… The loop is working exactly as designed; the app is still broken.” B (m-05): “There’s no give-up point… Nobody is told unless your team has set up an alert.” C (m-06): “A crashing container is restarted in place, in the same Pod on the same node…”

Engineers’ dashboards show this as CrashLoopBackOff; the Kubernetes docs’ own example shows a Pod at 200 restarts over two days.

“notifications keeps crash-looping, but Kubernetes keeps restarting it.”Ari
APPLY · Check 09 / 09 · k-09 · think, then reveal
Ari says: “notifications keeps crash-looping, but Kubernetes keeps restarting it.” What would you ask? Think of one or two questions, then show the answer.

Model answer: “So Kubernetes is doing its job. What’s wrong with the app? And is restarting hiding the problem from us?”

Why: the loop compares one thing, a running container, and acts on it; it can’t see the missing setting, so restarting is all it can do. And a service that restarts quietly can look “handled” on a dashboard while texts and emails stop going out. Chapter 12 comes back to how teams catch that. Under it: “If your question was ‘can Kubernetes fix it?’, that’s the trap this chapter is about: the loops restore the count; they don’t repair the app.”

Recap · .cp-recap · “In short”
  • With Kubernetes you write down what you want, the desired state, in manifests. Applying one records the intention; it doesn’t run anything.
  • Many small loops each compare one part of the record with what they observe, and act to close the gap, forever. No part plans for the others; work passes between them through the record.
  • Kubernetes runs Pods, and Pods are disposable: a dead node’s Pods are replaced by new ones, in minutes with default settings, with nobody awake. The app has to tolerate that, and the loops can’t fix a broken app.
  • Your provider runs the control plane. What goes into the record, and whether your app survives being replaced, stays yours.
